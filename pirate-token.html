How you can tell

The tell

Why Blortzen's numbers are the real ones — you don't have to trust either source. Compare the shapes.

Put the two ladders side by side and they have the same shape: gizmos at about 1 in 100, cosmetics in the hundreds, the vehicle in the thousands, and the token and gold outfit alone at the top.

At the bottom of the ladder — the end any player can check from their own games — the two nearly agree. Blortzen's any-treasure rate is ~1 in 100; Epic's whole published table sums to ~1 in 62, with the gizmo at 1 in 127. Same neighborhood.

Then the gap explodes at exactly one place: the top, where nobody can verify anything. That's the signature of obscuring — honest where it's falsifiable, stretched where it isn't. Scaring people about the jackpot also happens to sell the grind.

The derivation

For the nerds. This section is fully skippable — the answers are already at the top. This is just how the gold outfit number was forced out of the two sources.

Nobody has ever published the gold outfit's real odds. Blortzen got its joint wrong, and Epic published its figure on the obscured scale. But Epic's table keeps honest internal proportions — and a proportion survives rescaling. So the real number falls out two independent ways:

Route 1 — The proportion

  1. 1Inside Epic's own published table: gold outfit 1 in 1,562,500, token 1 in 137,500.
  2. 2Divide them: the outfit is 11.36× rarer than the token. That ratio is Epic's own — it holds on any scale.
  3. 3Apply the proportion to the real token rate — Blortzen's measured 1 in 2,000.

2,000 × 11.36 = 1 IN 22,727

Route 2 — Unscale Epic

  1. 1The token's obscuring factor: Epic's 137,500 ÷ the real 2,000 = 68.75×.
  2. 2Take Epic's gold outfit figure and divide out that same factor.

1,562,500 ÷ 68.75 = 1 IN 22,727
